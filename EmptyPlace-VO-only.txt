Every Sunday for twenty years, Winifred Holloway set two places at a table meant for one, and every Sunday the town of Sweetwater found that a little funnier and a little sadder, until it had settled into the kind of joke a town tells about a woman it has decided is harmless and mad.

She would lay the second plate the way you lay a plate for someone you expect — squared to the table edge, the fork just so, a cup turned right side up. She would put out a second chair, a small one, a child's chair she had never let go of though the child it was made for had not sat in it in two decades. And she would cook for two out of a cupboard that could barely feed one, and she would sit, and she would wait through the meal getting cold across from her, and then she would scrape the untouched plate back into the pot for the week's soup, because Winnie Holloway was poor to the bone and even a mad woman's hope has to answer to the price of flour.

The town knew the story, or thought it did. Everybody in Sweetwater knew that old Winnie who scrubbed the church floors and took in the washing had once had a boy, and that the boy was gone, and that she had never in twenty years stopped saying he was coming home. Some of them were kind about it. The women would leave her a little extra at the back door and not mention the plate. Some of them were not kind. The men at the saloon had a name for her Sunday ritual, and boys dared each other to peek in her window at suppertime to see the crazy washerwoman talking to an empty chair, and when they ran off laughing she never chased them, she only got up and drew the curtain, gentle, the way you close a door on a cold night.

Because here is the thing the town of Sweetwater had gotten wrong, the way towns get things wrong — comfortably, and for a long time, and all together.

Winnie Holloway was not mad. And her boy had not run off, and he had not died. Her boy had been *taken*.

(re-hook) Taken in the daylight, in front of witnesses, and carried east in a fine man's coach, by the very man who now sat in the biggest house in Sweetwater and tipped his hat to Winnie on the street with a smile like a closed drawer. The town had watched it happen twenty years ago and then, being a town, had found it more comfortable to remember it as a poor woman losing her wits than as a thing they had all stood by and allowed. Stay with me. Because twenty years is a long time to set a place for someone. But it is not, as this town was about to learn on a bright Sunday in the fall, one single day longer than a certain kind of love is willing to wait. Somebody was coming up the Sweetwater road. Stay for the knock.

[ACT 1]

To understand what was taken from her, you have to understand what she had, because she did not always scrub floors, and the small chair was not always empty.

Winnie had married Thomas Holloway at twenty, and they had been, by the plain measures that are the only ones that matter, happy. Thomas was a farrier and a fair hand with a bit of land at the edge of Sweetwater, not a prosperous man but a good one, with a laugh and a gentleness and a way of singing under his breath when he worked that Winnie could find him by across two fields. And when their son was born they named him Jonah, and Thomas made him a little chair with his own hands, sized for a small boy to sit up at the grown-up table, sanded smooth so the child would get no splinters, and he carved into the back of it a little running horse, because Jonah loved the horses in his father's paddock more than anything on God's earth and would stand at the rail for hours with his fat hand out, fearless, laughing, while the big animals lipped at his palm.

It was a small life and a poor one and it was, while it lasted, full to the brim. Winnie could close her eyes twenty years later and have the whole of it back in a breath: the smell of the bread she baked on Thursdays, the sound of Thomas singing low over a hoof in the lean-to, the particular weight of a sleeping six-year-old carried in from the paddock at dusk with hay in his hair. Jonah was a fearless child, a laugher, a boy who felt about horses the way other children feel about Christmas, and Thomas would set him on the top rail and stand behind with a hand at his back, and the two of them would watch the animals come up in the blue evening, and Winnie would watch the two of them from the kitchen door and think, in the plain unguarded way of a happy woman, that she had gotten more than her share and had better be grateful for it. She was grateful for it. That is the thing she held onto hardest, in the years after, on the nights the not-having threatened to take her mind for true — that she had not wasted the having. That she had known, while she held it, that it was everything.

Jonah was six years old the winter everything came apart.

It came apart the way it came apart for a great many families on the frontier, which is to say suddenly and through no fault of their own. There was a sickness that year — a fever that went through the stock first and then through the people — and Thomas Holloway, tending his neighbors' animals because that was the kind of man he was, took it, and was dead inside of a week, in the cold heart of a winter that had already emptied the smokehouse. And Winnie was left a widow at twenty-eight with a six-year-old boy, a played-out bit of land under a mortgage, and a fever still in the county that meant no work and no help and no mercy from the season.

The mortgage was held by Malcolm Radcliffe.

(re-hook) Now, I want you to hold that name, because he is the villain of this piece and he is a particular kind of villain, the kind this frontier bred in great numbers and the kind that is with us yet — not a man who robs you with a gun, but a man who robs you with a signature, and a smile, and the full protection of the law, and then sleeps like a baby because he never once broke a rule he hadn't first arranged to have written in his favor. Malcolm Radcliffe was about to do the single cruelest thing one human being can do to another, and he was about to do it *legally*, and that is why it worked, and that is why it stood for twenty years. Watch how it's done. It matters that you understand exactly how it was done.

Malcolm Radcliffe owned the bank in Sweetwater, and through the bank he owned a good deal of the town, and he was a big soft-spoken man who wore black broadcloth and quoted Scripture and had, in the place where other men keep a conscience, a ledger. When Thomas Holloway died owing him on the land, Radcliffe came to the little farmhouse — himself, in person, which the neighbors took for kindness and which was in fact something colder, because Malcolm Radcliffe never sent a man to do a thing he could enjoy doing himself. He sat in Winnie's kitchen, in Thomas's chair, and he laid it out for her gently, sorrowfully, in that closed-drawer voice, that the debt was the debt, that a widow with no man and no crop could never hope to pay it, that the bank would of course have to take the land — but that he, Malcolm Radcliffe, being a Christian man, had thought of a way to spare her the worst of it.

He and his wife back east had no children of their own, he said. It was a grief to them. And here was this fine strong healthy boy, this Jonah, headed for nothing but a poorhouse or worse with a penniless widowed mother — why, if Winnie would sign the boy over to the Radcliffes to raise, to be given a name and an education and a future no washerwoman could ever provide, then Malcolm Radcliffe would forgive the debt entire, clear the mortgage, and let her keep a corner of the land and her freedom besides. He said it was the boy's *best chance*. He said a good mother would want what was best for her son over what was easy for herself. He said it kindly. He said it the way the Devil says things, which is to say he wrapped the theft of a child in the language of a mother's love, so that if she refused she would be the selfish one.

And Winnie Holloway said no. She said no with a fury that frightened him, this small worn woman in a cold kitchen; she said she would sooner starve on that land with her boy than eat a king's bread without him; she said get out, get out of Thomas's chair and out of my house, you will not have my son. And Malcolm Radcliffe stood up, and brushed off his broadcloth, and said, in the doorway, not unkindly, that she should think it over, because the law was the law and the debt was the debt, and he would be back when the note came due, and there were only so many ways this could go for a woman with nothing.

(re-hook) He was patient. That is the thing about his kind, the thing that makes them so hard to fight — they have time, and you do not. He let the winter do his work for him. He let the note come due. And when it did, and Winnie could not pay it, because of course she could not pay it, no power on earth could have paid it, he did not send the boy east as a mercy. He took him as a *creditor*. Stay with me. This is the part the town watched, and this is the part the town spent twenty years pretending it had misremembered.

The morning they took Jonah, there was frost on the paddock rail where the boy used to feed the horses. Radcliffe came with a paper and with the sheriff — a weak man who did what the bank told him — and the paper was lawful, foreclosure and a writ and words Winnie couldn't read fast enough to fight, and the long and the short of it was that the land was the bank's now, and the widow was a trespasser on it, and the only thing Malcolm Radcliffe was offering, still, in his Christian mercy, was the same bargain: the boy for the debt, the boy for a roof, the boy for her own survival. Only now it wasn't a bargain. Now it was the sheriff standing in the yard, and a coach waiting, and a fine cold woman in the coach who was to be the boy's new mother, and a mad scramble and a child screaming for his mama and a mother's arms being *held* — physically held, by two men, while her son was carried past her into a stranger's coach — and the town of Sweetwater standing at the edge of the yard and along the road, watching, not one of them stepping forward, because Malcolm Radcliffe held paper on half of them and the other half were afraid, and it is a terrible thing how much cruelty a crowd will permit if the cruelty comes with a lawful paper and a soft voice.

The last thing Jonah Holloway saw of his mother, through the little window of a rich man's coach as it pulled away, was Winnie on her knees in the frost of the paddock she'd lost, both arms still reaching down the road after him, screaming a name the coach was already too far to hear.

And they took him east, and they gave him the Radcliffe name, and they told him — this is the cruelty inside the cruelty — they told the boy that his mother had *given him up*. That she was too poor and too tired to want him. That she had signed him away and taken the money and been glad of it. They told a grieving six-year-old that his mama hadn't loved him enough to keep him, and they told it to him over and over across the years until a boy who had been carried screaming from his mother's arms grew into a young man who believed, in the private broken place we all keep, that he had been unwanted. And Winnie, back in Sweetwater with a corner of lost land and no way to read where a coach goes or hire a man to chase it or fight a bank that owned the sheriff — Winnie had no way on earth to reach him, to tell him the truth, to send one word east to say *I did not give you up, they tore you from me, I have never for one hour stopped*.

So she did the only thing a powerless woman could do. She stayed. She would not leave Sweetwater, not for anything, not ever, because Sweetwater was the one place on the whole wide earth where her boy knew to look for her if he ever came looking. She took in washing and scrubbed the church and lived on soup and pennies. And every Sunday, she set his place.

She was not entirely without a friend in it. There was a neighbor woman, Effie Dell, a widow herself and no better off, who had stood at the edge of that frozen yard the morning they took Jonah and had gone home and been sick with shame and had never, in twenty years, gotten it out of her. Effie could not fight a bank any more than Winnie could. But she could leave a wrapped half a loaf on Winnie's back step of a Saturday night, and she could sit with her some Sunday evenings across that terrible second plate and not say a word about it, and she could be, in a town that had decided Winnie was a joke, the one person who looked at the set place and the small chair and understood that she was in the presence not of madness but of a faith too large for the town to hold. "He's coming home, Effie," Winnie would say, quiet, turning the child's cup right side up. And Effie Dell, who did not know if it was true and feared it wasn't, would answer the only way love lets you answer a thing like that. "I know it, Winnie," she'd say. "I know he is." And she would help her friend clear two plates, and go home, and pray she hadn't lied.

[ACT 2]

Twenty years is a long time, and a town's memory is short and self-forgiving, and by the time this story truly opens the thing that had been done to Winnie Holloway had been sanded down, in the town's telling, into something it could live beside.

They did not say *we stood by while a banker had a child torn from his mother for a debt*. They said *poor Winnie lost her boy young and it turned her mind*. They did not say *Malcolm Radcliffe bought himself a son with a foreclosure*. They said *Mr. Radcliffe took in an orphan out of Christian charity, and hasn't he done well by the town, look at the bank, look at the new church roof he paid for*. Malcolm Radcliffe had prospered mightily in twenty years. He was the richest man in the county now, a pillar, a name, an old man in fine black broadcloth who sat in the front pew every Sunday not forty feet from the woman scrubbing the floor he walked in on, and if he ever thought of her at all it was as a small settled account, a bit of unpleasantness long since closed, a mad old washerwoman who set a plate for a ghost.

He did not know that the ghost was coming home.

Because here is what had happened in the wide world beyond Sweetwater, unknown to the town and unknown to Winnie, across those twenty years. The Radcliffes' cold marriage had curdled; the fine woman in the coach had never warmed to the stolen boy, and Malcolm Radcliffe back east was a different and harder man than Malcolm Radcliffe lording it over a small town, and the boy they had renamed grew up in a big cold house that was rich in everything but the one thing he'd been carried screaming away from. But the boy was clever, and the boy was strong, and the boy — whatever they told him about his mother — carried something they could not beat or buy out of him: a memory. A paddock rail. A big gentle hand teaching his small one to hold flat for a horse's lip. A woman's voice singing under her breath. The smell of bread. And the name they'd tried to paint over, the name he half remembered being screamed down a frozen road, a name that wasn't the Radcliffe name at all.

They raised him in a tall cold house with servants and lessons and everything a boy could be given except the one thing, and the fine woman who was to have been his mother could not make herself love a child bought to fill a hole in a marriage, and so she did not try, and Malcolm Radcliffe was away at his affairs, and the boy grew up in a house full of rooms and empty of arms. What they gave him instead of love was the lie, administered steadily, the way you'd give a bitter medicine — that his mother had signed him away, that she'd been glad of the money, that he was a charity case who ought to be grateful and was, if anything, ungrateful. A younger child might have swallowed it whole. But Jonah had been six, not two; he had memories, and he kept them the way a starving man keeps a crust, in secret, taking them out at night: the rail, the horses, the singing, the bread, and under all of it, growing fainter each year and never quite gone, the sound of a woman screaming a name down a frozen road — his name, his *real* name, not the one they'd painted over him — and it did not match, that scream, it did not match a mother who'd been glad. A boy can be told a thing a thousand times and still, in the deep place, not believe it, if once, at the bottom of him, he has been loved for true. They had lied to him for years. They had never quite been able to finish the job, because Winnie had done her work too well in six short years, and love laid down that early does not scrub out.

(re-hook) He got free of them the first day he could — walked out at seventeen with nothing, the way his father had once walked the roads, and made his own way west. And he studied law, because a man who has watched his whole life get taken by a lawful paper grows up wanting to understand the paper. He was brilliant at it, and he was relentless, and somewhere in those years he did a quiet, enormous thing: he dug his true name back up out of the records they'd buried it under, and he took it for his own again, Holloway, so that whatever he became he would become it as *her* son and not theirs. He made himself, by his own hand, into a man of consequence in the territory. But through all of it, through every year of it, one question sat in him like a splinter under the skin: *was it true?* Had she given him up? Because if a man is going to go looking for the truth of the worst thing he believes about himself, he had better first become strong enough to survive the answer — and it had taken Jonah Holloway twenty years to get strong enough. Stay with me. He is almost to Sweetwater now. And he does not yet know which he is riding toward — a mother who wanted him, or the confirmation that she never did.

He came into the territory as a circuit judge — appointed, respected, a hard clean name in a country short of them — and he had a docket that took him from town to town, and he had, folded in his breast pocket across every mile of it, a thing he had spent years and no small money assembling: the truth. Because a judge can pull a thread that a washerwoman cannot. Jonah had gone back, on paper, through the whole of it — the foreclosure, the writ, the bank's records, the old sheriff's file, a deathbed statement he'd tracked down from the very woman who'd ridden in that coach to be his cold new mother and had confessed, at the end of her loveless life, what had truly been done and how a screaming child had been lied to. The deathbed statement was the thing that finally broke it open. The cold woman from the coach — his purchased mother, who had never warmed to him and whom he had not seen in years — was dying back east, and something in the nearness of the end had loosened what a whole loveless life had held shut, and she had sent for a lawyer and made a statement, plain and terrible, of what had truly been done in a frozen Sweetwater yard: that the boy had not been given but taken, that the mother had fought and been held, that they had lied to the child on purpose and kept lying because the truth, once a boy was grown, was too dangerous to let stand. She had signed it. Whether she did it out of a late-arriving conscience or only to be rid of the weight before she met her Maker, Jonah never knew and never much cared; what mattered was that it was signed, and witnessed, and it said in a dead woman's own words the sentence he had waited twenty years to be allowed to believe: *the mother did not give the boy up.*

He had it all, then. He knew, by the time his circuit brought him at last to the town of Sweetwater on a bright Sunday in the fall, that his mother had not given him up. He knew she had been held down while they carried him off. He knew she had lost the land and the fight and every earthly means of reaching him. What he carried into Sweetwater was not a hope any longer. It was a certainty, folded in a breast pocket, that the worst thing he had ever believed about himself was a lie a rich man had told him — and under the certainty, trembling, the one thing paper could not settle: whether he had come home too late to say so to her face.

What he did not know — what no record in any courthouse could tell him — was whether she was still alive. Whether, after twenty years, there would be anyone at all in Sweetwater to come home to.

(re-hook) So the judge did a thing that surprised his clerk. He did not ride into Sweetwater in his fine coat with his fine escort and take the good rooms at the hotel. He got down off the noon stage a mile out, alone, in a plain coat, and he walked into the town of his birth on foot, quiet, a stranger, the way his mother had once taught a small boy to walk up to a nervous horse — slow, and gentle, and with your hand open where it can be seen. He walked past the bank Malcolm Radcliffe built with the price of him. He walked past the church his mother scrubbed. And he began, heart in his mouth, to ask a careful question of the town of Sweetwater: whether anyone here remembered a woman named Holloway.

The first person he asked was a boy outside the mercantile, and the boy's answer put the judge's hand flat against the wall of the building for a moment, to steady himself.

"Old mad Winnie?" the boy said, cheerful, the way children are cheerful about the cruelties they've inherited without examining. "Sure, everybody knows Winnie. She's the washerwoman. She's touched — she sets a place every Sunday for a boy that run off on her a hundred years ago and talks to it like he's there. Ma says it's sad. The big fellows say it's a hoot. You can go look in her window at supper if you want, everybody does." And the boy pointed down a side street, to the poorest end of Sweetwater, where a small mean house sat with its curtain not yet drawn against the evening. "She's likely laying it out right now. It's Sunday."

The judge — Jonah Holloway, come twenty years and a thousand miles home — stood very still in the street with the boy's cheerful words going through him like the two halves of a struck bell. *She sets a place every Sunday.* For twenty years, a town had watched his mother set a place for him every single Sunday and had turned it into a joke to frighten children with. She had waited. Through everything, believing him alive, believing he would come, waiting in the one place he knew to find her, she had never once stopped setting his place — and the town had called her *mad* for the truest and most faithful love any of them would ever be near.

And something happened in the judge then that had not happened since he was six years old in a cold coach. His careful, courtroom, twenty-years-armored composure cracked straight down the middle, and Jonah Holloway put his hand over his mouth like a man holding something in, and turned his face to the wall of the mercantile, and wept — silently, terribly, the way a grown man weeps when a grief he has carried so long it became furniture suddenly stands up and walks toward him alive.

Winnie Holloway had the plate squared to the edge and the fork just so and was reaching to turn the child's cup right side up when the knock came.

She did not startle. Knocks came, sometimes, Sunday evenings — boys, mostly, run off laughing before she reached the door. She wiped her hands on her apron out of long habit, and she went, and she opened it, expecting the empty step and the sound of young feet fleeing in the dusk.

There was a man on the step. A grown man in a plain dark coat, tall, with a strong lined face she did not know, standing very still with his hat in his two hands the way a person stands who is afraid of frightening you. The last of the daylight was behind him and she could not see him well, and she waited, polite, worn, for him to state his business.

And the man on the step opened his mouth, and no words came, and he tried again, and what came out at last, in a voice that shook like a much younger voice, was one word. It was not *ma'am*. It was not *Mrs. Holloway*. It was the word a six-year-old screams down a frozen road. It was the word carved into no document and printed in no ledger and beaten out of no boy no matter how they tried.

"Mama?"

Winnie Holloway went absolutely still.

For twenty years she had built her whole life on a certainty the entire town had told her was madness, and in twenty years not one single soul had said that word to her, and she had never — not once, not on the worst nights — let herself imagine the sound of it, because to imagine it was a door she could not have survived opening for nothing. And now it hung in the dusk of her own doorway in a stranger's shaking voice, and she stood there with her chapped hand on the door and looked up into the face she could not see clearly, and she did the thing that broke every person who later told this story, which they all did, for the rest of their lives.

She lifted her worn hand, slowly, the way you reach toward something that might not be real, and she laid it against the grown man's cheek. And her thumb found, at the corner of his jaw, a small old scar — a scar she knew, a scar from a paddock rail and a fall and a afternoon twenty-six years gone when a fearless laughing two-year-old had climbed where he shouldn't and his mother had cleaned the cut with her apron and kissed it and told him he was brave. Her thumb found that scar in the dark the way a blind woman finds a word in a book she has read ten thousand times.

"Jonah," she said. Not a question. She had never once doubted. "Jonah. You came home. I set your place. I set it every Sunday. I knew you'd — " and then the twenty years took her voice, and it did not matter, because her son had already gone down onto his knees on her poor doorstep and put his face against her apron the way he had when he was small, and wrapped his arms around his mother, this judge, this hard clean name in a hard country, and become, in the space of one word, a boy who had finally, finally come home — and the two of them knelt there in the doorway holding on, twenty years of held-down arms finally closing around what had been carried off, and neither of them could speak, and neither of them needed to, because some tables are worth setting for twenty years, and some doors are worth waiting a whole life to open, and this was both.

(re-hook) They will tell you, the people of Sweetwater — and by morning the whole town knew, and by the next Sunday half of them found a reason to walk past the poor end of town — they will tell you that the old washerwoman and the circuit judge sat up the whole of that first night at a small table with a child's chair pulled to it, and that she fed him from a cupboard he quietly saw to it was never bare again, and that he told her the truth of the twenty years and she told him the truth of them, and the two truths reached across the table and finally met. But the story is not quite finished, because there was still a man in the biggest house in Sweetwater who had built his whole life on the belief that this account was closed. And a judge, it happens, is a very bad enemy for a man whose fortune was founded on a lawful paper. Stay for it. You have earned the reckoning too.

Malcolm Radcliffe learned that the circuit judge was Winnie Holloway's son the way the powerful always learn such things — too late, and from a frightened underling, and with the ground already gone from under him. He had spent twenty years secure in the knowledge that what he'd done was *legal*, that every signature was in order, that no court in the territory could touch a foreclosure that had been papered so carefully. And that was true, as far as it went. But Jonah Holloway had not spent twenty years and a fortune learning the law in order to reopen a foreclosure. He had learned the law in order to understand, precisely, all the *other* things a man like Radcliffe does around the edges of a lawful theft — the pressure on a weak sheriff, the records adjusted, the later dealings, the decades of a powerful man's careful cutting of corners in a town too frightened to keep an honest ledger — and a judge who arrives in a county and quietly opens the books of its richest man, with cause, and with the cold personal patience of someone who has waited twenty years, will find what there is to find. And there is always something to find.

I will not walk you through the whole unwinding of Malcolm Radcliffe, because it took the better part of two years and it was done in courtrooms and not in a single grand scene, which is how real reckonings mostly come — slowly, on paper, the same weapon he'd used, turned. But the shape of it was this: the pillar came down. The dealings came to light. The town that had called the man a benefactor watched the county learn what its benefactor was, and the fortune built on a stolen boy dispersed into fines and judgments and the pockets of people he'd quietly robbed for decades, and Malcolm Radcliffe died a few years on in a much smaller house, having learned the one thing his kind never expects to learn, which is that some accounts are not closed just because you have decided to stop looking at them — that a debt can sit twenty years, patient as a mother at a Sunday table, and still, in the end, come due.

[CLOSING]

But that is the smaller half of the ending, and I would not have you leave on it, because Malcolm Radcliffe was never the point. He was only the weather the love had to survive.

And I will tell you one more small thing about the town, because it belongs to the ending. Effie Dell lived to see it. The neighbor woman who had left half-loaves on a mad friend's step for twenty years, who had said *I know he's coming, Winnie* on a hundred Sunday evenings without daring to believe it — Effie was there the week the whole town understood at last what it had mocked, and she sat in Winnie's new warm kitchen with a cup she didn't have to bring her own bread to fill, and the two old women looked at each other across a table that finally got eaten from, and did not need to say the thing, because they had already said it every Sunday for twenty years. It turns out that when you tell a grieving friend *I know he's coming home* for two decades on nothing but love, you are not lying. You are only early.

The point is the table.

Jonah Holloway did not take his mother away to a fine house in a far city, though he could have, and offered to, and she thought about it for exactly as long as it took to laugh. Sweetwater was where she had waited for him; she was not going to be chased out of it now that the waiting was done, and besides, she said, a body couldn't just leave a church floor half scrubbed. So the judge did a thing that reshaped his whole life around a small table at the poor end of a small town: he made Sweetwater the center of his circuit, and he built his mother a good house — not a grand one, she wouldn't have it, but a warm dry good one — on the very corner of the very land that had been taken from her, which he bought back with a lawful paper of his own and put in her name, so that Winnie Holloway ended her days on the ground where Thomas had sung under his breath and a small boy had fed the horses, a widow no longer poor and no longer alone and never again, as long as she lived, called mad by anyone in that town, because there is nothing that cures a town of its cruelty faster than watching the thing it mocked turn out to be the truest thing in it.

And every Sunday — every single Sunday, for all the years they had left — the circuit judge came home to Sweetwater if the whole territory had to wait on his docket, and he sat down at his mother's table in the small chair's grown-up neighbor, and Winnie Holloway set two places, the way she always had. Only now the second one got eaten from. Now the plate went back to the cupboard clean because a big gray-templed man had cleared it, telling his mother about his week, while she watched him eat with the particular bottomless attention of a woman who spent twenty years memorizing an empty chair and now got to fill her eyes with the miracle of it filled. She kept the little chair, too, the one Thomas made, with the running horse carved in the back. It sat by the hearth all those years, and when in time there were grandchildren — and there were — it was pulled to the table for them, and it turned out a chair built by a farrier's loving hands for a boy who loved horses had been waiting, all along, just like Winnie, for the family to come back and fill it.

The frontier was full of the powerful taking what they wanted from the powerless and calling it law, and calling it charity, and sleeping soundly. But it was full, too, quieter and less remarked, of a different kind of strength — the strength of a worn woman at a poor table who would not, would not, *would not* stop believing in the return of what was stolen from her, no matter how long the road or how loud the mockery, and who by the sheer stubborn faithfulness of her waiting turned out to be the sanest soul in Sweetwater and the richest, in the end, in the only wealth that was ever worth the name. They called her mad for setting a place for twenty years. She was not mad. She was a mother. And a mother, it turns out, keeps better books than any banker alive: she knew, to the day, that she was owed a son, and she never once wrote the debt off, and she lived to collect it in full, in the sound of one word in a doorway at dusk.
