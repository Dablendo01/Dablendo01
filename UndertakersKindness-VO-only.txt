Five weeks after they put the nameless old beggar in the ground, a lawyer in a fine black coach rolled into the town of Granite Flat, walked into the saloon with the dust of six hundred miles on his boots, and asked one question that made every man in the room go quiet.

"I'm looking for the grave of an old man who died here in the spring," he said. "A stranger. No money, no people, no name anyone knew. I'm told he's buried on the hill." He set his hat on the bar, and he looked around that room slow, the way a man looks when he already knows more than he's asking. "What I want to know is — who paid for the headstone?"

Now, you have to understand what the town of Granite Flat knew, and what it didn't.

It knew there was a grave. It knew the old tramp had come through in April, sick and shaking, with a rough patched coat and no bedroll and a smell of long roads on him, and it knew the hotel had turned him out and the saloon had turned him out and decent folks had crossed the street when he came shuffling down the boardwalk. It knew he had died — somewhere, in somebody's back room — and it knew, because the whole town had laughed about it for a month, that Abel Thorne, the undertaker, the big slow crow of a man who buried Granite Flat's dead, had done a thing so foolish it had become the town's favorite joke.

Abel Thorne had buried the beggar like a rich man.

Real coffin — good pine, planed smooth, lined with clean linen. He'd washed the old man's body and combed the fringe of his white hair and dressed him for the grave in Abel's own Sunday coat, the only good coat Abel Thorne owned. He'd hired no one and asked no one; he'd dug the grave himself on the best corner of the hill, where the light comes long in the evening, and he'd stood over it alone with his hat off, reading the words, because his one unbreakable rule was that nobody in his care went into the ground alone. And then — this was the part the town howled at — the man had spent his last twelve dollars, the whole of what stood between the undertaker and empty, on a slab of good granite, and he'd carved it himself, letter by letter, by lamplight, over nine nights. The town said it to his face at the end of it: You are the poorest man in Granite Flat, Abel Thorne, and you carved a monument to a tramp.

Because here is what the town of Granite Flat did not know.

The old beggar's name was Amos Caldwell. And Amos Caldwell was the richest man in the territory — richer than the bank, richer than the railroad men, a silver king who had walked away from a mansion and mines worth a kingdom, and who had spent his last months on earth shuffling through hard little towns in a patched coat, dying by inches, watching what each one did with an old man worth nothing. There was a will. There was a lawyer in a distant city, holding it, with instructions to follow the letters Amos mailed and see where the road ended. And the last line of that will was about to fall on Granite Flat like a hammer on an anvil.

(re-hook) Because in the saloon that day, when the lawyer asked who paid for the headstone, a very fine gentleman in a silver-buttoned vest stepped forward, smiling his best civic smile — and he was going to claim the credit for the whole town, and mostly for himself. And the one man who had actually done it, the man who was owed everything, was up on the hill at that very moment, digging a pauper's grave for free, knowing none of it. Stay with me. Because the question wasn't idle, the lawyer wasn't a fool, and the dead man had left behind one small brass proof that no liar in Granite Flat could answer for. It's going to be worth the wait.

To understand what Abel Thorne did, you have to understand what Granite Flat thought of him — and what it cost him to be who he was.

He was a big man, Abel, heavy through the shoulders, with large careful hands and a slow deep voice he hardly used, and he had been the undertaker in Granite Flat for eleven years, which meant the town needed him the way it needed the gravedigger's shovel and liked him about as much. Children were told to hush when his wagon passed. Men fell silent at the bar when he came in for his one beer, and found reasons to drift. Women watched him go by in his rusty black coat — a coat gone brown at the seams with age, because a new one cost money and Abel's money had a way of turning into other people's coffins — and they called him the crow, not always behind his hand. He was forty-five years old. He ate his supper alone over his workbench nearly every night of his life, in a plank building at the end of the street that was workshop and parlor and home all in one, with the sawdust smell of coffin pine in everything he owned.

It hadn't always been that way. There had been a wife.

Her name was Lila, and she'd had a laugh you could hear from the street, and for six years the undertaker's grim house had been, of all the buildings in Granite Flat, the warm one. She died in the fever year, along with forty more — died in three days, in the middle of the dying, when the town was so afraid of the sickness that no one would come near the houses it had touched. Abel built her coffin himself, with his own planes and his own hands, in the middle of the night, weeping into the sawdust. And when he buried her, on the hill, in the rain — no one came. Not one soul out of a town she had fed and nursed and laughed with. They were afraid, and fear makes people small, and Abel Thorne stood over his wife's grave utterly alone and read the words himself into the wind.

He never held it against them. That was the thing about Abel that the town never did understand — he wasn't bitter, he was just changed. Something got decided in him that day in the rain, and it came out afterward as a rule, and the rule was this: nobody goes into the ground alone. Not while Abel Thorne was undertaker. The ones with families got his best work at a fair price. And the ones with nobody — the tramps found frozen in the wash, the miners with no kin on the books, the girls from the row houses that decent Granite Flat wouldn't name in daylight — those, Abel buried right, at his own cost, with a washed body and a plank coffin and a read verse and his hat in his hands. Every one. Eleven years.

(re-hook) The county paid him four dollars a pauper, when it paid at all, and a burial done Abel's way cost nine or ten, and you can do that arithmetic as fast as the town did. It's why the undertaker was the poorest man on the street. It's also why, when the sick old stranger came shuffling into Granite Flat that raw April evening, there was exactly one door in town that was going to open for him. But before that door, he had to be turned away from all the others — and the man doing the turning is somebody you need to meet. Watch him close. He matters at the end.

Vernon Price owned the hotel, the general store, and — the way he saw it — the town. He was chairman of the town council, president of the civic improvement society, and the possessor of the finest vest buttons in Granite Flat, real silver, which he wore the way other men wear a gun. He gave speeches about progress. He had a plan, much discussed, to bring investors up from the city and make Granite Flat "the jewel of the county," and he ran the town with an oiled smile and a ledger where he kept, in neat columns, everything and everyone by their price. Vernon Price was not cruel the way a brawler is cruel. He was cruel the way winter is — by simple, total indifference to anything that couldn't pay.

And he had a particular contempt for Abel Thorne, because the undertaker offended his arithmetic. A man who spent money on the dead poor was, in Price's ledger, a leak. Worse — the pauper's field on the hill sat on ground Price wanted, a gentle slope with the best view in town, which he had promised, quiet-like, to his city investors for a fine hotel. For a year now he'd been working the council: move the paupers' bones to a ditch lot by the flats, cancel the county burial contract, ease that big slow fool out entirely and bring in a cut-rate man from the city who'd plant the poor like fence posts and never waste a nickel on linen. "Sentiment," Vernon Price liked to say, tapping his cane on the boardwalk, "is the one debt this town cannot afford."

That was the Granite Flat the old man walked into on the ninth of April, with the wind raw off the flats and the light going purple, an old man small and wiry and bent, in a drover's coat patched with three kinds of cloth, shaking with something more than cold.

(re-hook) He tried the hotel first. Remember that he tried the hotel first — remember it at the end, when the man who owned it stands up in the saloon to collect a dead man's gratitude. Here is what actually happened at that door.

Amos Caldwell — though no one in Granite Flat would ever hear that name while he lived — climbed the three steps of the Price Hotel slow, holding the rail, and stood dripping road-dust on the clean porch, and asked the clerk, in a voice gone thin as paper, whether there was a corner where an old man could lie down out of the wind. He had no money, he said, but he could sweep, or sit up nights with the stove. The clerk went and got Vernon Price, and Vernon Price came out with his thumbs in his silver-buttoned vest, looked the old man over once, top to bottom, the way he priced everything, and found the sum wanting.

"This is a business, old fellow," Price said, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse — that smooth, reasonable, sorrowful tone men like Price keep for the poor. "Not a mission. If we bedded every vagrant who liked our porch, we'd be a poorhouse by June. You'll find the road out of town keeps going in both directions." And then, to the clerk, in a voice not quite low enough: "See he doesn't linger. Nothing empties a lobby like the smell of poverty."

The old man took it without a word. He'd taken it in ten towns before this one; he had a way, by then, of receiving contempt like weather. He shuffled down the steps and tried the saloon, where the barman let him stand by the stove for exactly as long as it took the regulars to start their jokes and then moved him along. He tried the church; it was locked. He tried the livery; the boy there, not unkind but afraid for his job, turned him out of the straw. By full dark the old man was sitting on the steps of the last building on the street, a plank building with a lamp burning late in the window and a sign gone half unreadable with weather, and he was coughing in a way that came all the way up from his boots, and that is where the undertaker, coming out to fetch stovewood, nearly stumbled over him.

Abel looked down at the old man on his steps. The old man looked up at the big shape in the doorway, and past it, at the workshop inside — the sawhorses, the planed boards, the coffin standing finished in the lamplight — and he laughed, a dry rattle with no self-pity in it at all, and said the first thing anyone in Granite Flat had heard him say above a mumble:

"Well. I've been shown every door in this town, and it turns out the only one open is the undertaker's. There's a sermon in that, if either of us had the strength for it."

And Abel Thorne, who hardly used his voice, stood aside from his door and used it. "Come in out of the wind," he said. "I've got stew, and the stove's going. You can have the cot by the chimney." And when the old man reached the step and stumbled, Abel took his arm — took the arm of the shaking, road-filthy old tramp the whole town had spent a day refusing to touch — and helped him over the threshold like he was somebody.

[ACT 2]

The old man stayed six days, and they were, though neither of them said any such word, the best six days either man had passed in years.

Abel fed him stew and bread and put him on the cot by the chimney under his own spare blanket, and asked him nothing — not his name, not his people, not what he'd been. That was the undertaker's way; eleven years of burying strangers had taught him that a man's story is his own property, to give or keep. The old man gave only a little. Said his name was "Amos, just Amos." Said he'd "been in silver, once, and got out of it." Said he was going no place in particular, by way of everyplace, and was in no hurry, being nearly there. He had a dry, flinty humor that woke something in Abel that had been asleep since the fever year, and in the evenings the two of them sat by the stove, the undertaker and the tramp, and talked the slow talk of men who have both lost the thing they were living for and gone on living anyway.

Because they had that in common, and they smelled it on each other the first night. Abel told him — the first soul he'd ever told — about Lila, and the coffin planed at midnight, and the funeral in the rain that nobody came to. The old man listened all the way through without once saying it would be all right, which is the only way to listen to a thing like that. And on the fourth night, the old man paid it back. He'd had a son, he said, looking at the stove. A good one. Daniel. Lost him young, in a hole in the ground the old man had ordered dug — "my arithmetic," he said, "my hurry" — and a wife who died of the grief of it within the year. "I was rich, then," he said, and it was the only time he said the word. "I have spent a long while since trying to give it back. It don't give back. There's things you buy that money won't return."

He had one possession — one, in all the world, that he hadn't shed somewhere down the road: a brass pocket watch, old and plain, that he wound every night at the same hour with hands that shook worse each day. On the fifth night he pressed it on Abel, and would not be refused. "It keeps time true," he said, "which is more than most of us manage. When I'm gone — and we both know the schedule I'm on, we're neither of us fools — you keep it wound. Not buried. Wound. A watch in a coffin is a waste of a good watch, and besides —" and here the flinty humor came up through the fever one more time — "where I'm going, I expect they keep their own time."

(re-hook) Abel took the watch because a dying man's gift refuses badly. He did not open the back of it. He did not know what was engraved there, and he wouldn't know for five more weeks, and neither will you — but that watch is the hinge this whole story swings on, so keep your eye on the undertaker's vest pocket. Now comes the hard night. Stay with me; the morning after it is where the town earns what's coming to it.

On the sixth night the cough turned, the way a cough will in an old chest, and by midnight the fever was walking him out. Abel sat by the cot the whole way, because that was the rule in its truest form — nobody goes alone, not into the ground and not toward it — and kept the old man's hand in his big careful one, and kept the lamp lit, because dying in the dark is a fear that doesn't leave a man just because he's brave. Near the end, the old man came clear one last time, the way they sometimes do, and looked at the big homely worried face above him, and what he said — his last words in this world — was this:

"Ten towns, Abel. Ten towns I walked through with nothing, to see what nothing gets a man. And it got me told, and moved along, and priced, and laughed at — ten towns' worth. And then, in the eleventh, one door." His hand tightened on the undertaker's, thin as a bird's foot and strong as wire. "It was worth the whole walk to find one door. You go on being what you are, you hear me? The world will tell you it makes you a fool. The world has never once known what a thing is worth."

He died a little after four, quiet, with the lamp lit and his hand held, in the one building in Granite Flat that had wanted him.

And in the morning, Abel Thorne went to the county clerk to report a pauper's death, and Vernon Price — who kept the clerk's office in his vest pocket along with the rest of the town — heard about it before noon, and put it around the saloon by supper, already shaped as a joke: the old vagrant had crawled the length of town to die on the crow's doorstep. Trash finds the trash heap, Price said, tapping his cane, pleased with the line. Four dollars, county rate; a canvas sack and a ditch corner was the arithmetic. He said it in the hearing of half the town: "And I'll thank our undertaker not to waste linen on it. Sentiment is a debt this town cannot afford."

What Abel Thorne did next, he did in plain sight of all of them, over nine days, and he never argued with a soul about it. He just did it, the way weather does.

He built the coffin out of his good pine — the clear, straight boards he kept for paying customers — and planed it smooth as a church rail, and lined it with clean linen Cora Quinn sewed for him. Cora was the seamstress, a widow herself, quiet, with steady grey eyes; she'd been leaving her mending money in Abel's coffin linings at cost for years, for the paupers, and she was the one soul on the street who never called him crow. When he came to her for linen for a tramp's coffin and confessed he could pay her only slowly, she looked at him a long moment and charged him nothing at all, and if you want to know when the second thread of this story was tied, it was tied there, over a bolt of white cloth neither of them could afford.

He washed the old man's body and combed the white fringe of his hair. He dressed him — and the town truly did not forgive him this part for a month — in his own Sunday coat, the single good garment Abel Thorne owned, brushed and mended, because a man ought to meet whatever's next in a coat that fits him and doesn't have another man's patches on it. He tried first to tuck the brass watch into the coffin, and stood there a moment, and took it back out, because a promise is a promise: not buried. Wound.

He dug the grave himself, on the best corner of the hill — the southeast corner, where the light comes long in the evening and you can see the creek — six feet, square-sided, in ground still cold from winter. Vernon Price came up the hill personally to remind him that corner lots on that slope were "spoken for in the town's plans," and Abel, standing chest-deep in the ground with the shovel going, replied with the longest speech the town ever got out of him on the subject. "Dead's dead, Mr. Price," he said. "This one's mine to do right by. You'll want to stand back from the edge; the dirt's soft."

(re-hook) And then came the twelve dollars — the part of this story the town told as a joke for five weeks, and would have given ten years apiece, every man of them, to take back. Watch what the poorest man in Granite Flat does with the last money he has in the world.

Abel Thorne had twelve dollars and forty cents to his name; that was the whole of it, the entire estate of eleven years of burying a town, kept in a coffee tin over the workbench against the winter. There was a granite slab at the mason's yard in the county seat, hauled in two years back for a cattleman's monument and never paid for or claimed. Abel walked the nine miles, and paid twelve dollars for it, and hauled it back on the coffin wagon, and stood it in his workshop, and then, every night for nine nights, after the day's work, by lamplight, with a mallet and chisels and his big careful hands — the undertaker carved.

He was no mason. The letters came slow, and a little uneven, and truer for it. The town watched through his window — they made an entertainment of it; men brought their beer over from the saloon to watch the crow chisel a tramp's monument and offer wit through the glass — and Abel worked on as if they were weather too. Nine nights. And when he and Cora and the livery boy — the same boy who'd turned the old man out of the straw and had been sick about it ever since, and who Abel, knowing that, had quietly asked for help precisely so the boy could put it down — when the three of them raised that stone at the head of the grave on the hill, this is what Granite Flat read on it, and laughed:

HERE LIES A STRANGER
DIED APRIL 15
KNOWN TO GOD
HE WAS SOMEBODY'S SON

Four lines, carved by lamplight by a man who'd spent his last dollar on the granite. The three of them stood there in the evening light, and Abel read the words aloud, hat off, because nobody goes into the ground alone — and down in the town Vernon Price was already at work turning the whole affair to use.

Because the joke, Price found, had a useful edge on it. A man that foolish with money, he told the council, was a man who could not be trusted with the county's. The contract for pauper burials came up for renewal at the end of June, and Price had his cut-rate city man ready, and the vote was moving his way. More: the "improvement plan" for the hill — clear the pauper field, move the bones to the ditch lot by the flats — was suddenly wearing new clothes: dignity for the town, Price called it, a fine hotel where the trash graves stood. By the last week of May, Abel Thorne was five weeks from losing the contract that kept him fed, and the field where his paupers lay — where Lila lay, at the field's decent edge, in the plot beside the one Abel kept for himself — was five weeks from Vernon Price's surveyors. The undertaker had an empty coffee tin, a mortgaged workshop, a watch he kept wound out of a promise to a dead tramp, and the town's laughter for a coat.

That was the state of things — the kind man beaten, the smiling man winning, the arithmetic of Granite Flat working exactly the way Vernon Price's kind of arithmetic always seems to work — on the day the black coach came up the flats road.

It came in on a Tuesday, lacquered black, city-built, drawn by a matched pair, and Granite Flat had it in view for four miles of flat road, which was time enough for the whole street to find a reason to be on the boardwalk when it pulled up in front of the Price Hotel. The man who stepped down was thin and dry and sixty, in a black suit and spectacles, and he had the particular stillness of a profession that gets paid to notice things. He declined Vernon Price's hand-shaken welcome with perfect courtesy, took a room, asked one question of the clerk — the clerk told six people within the hour — and in the evening he walked into the saloon, set his hat on the bar, and asked it again, of everyone, in a voice that carried like a dropped coin.

"My name is Quincey Adair. I am an attorney, and I have come six hundred miles on the business of an estate. In April an old man died in this town — a stranger, penniless, no kin known. I'm told he lies on the hill under a granite marker." He let that sit, and his eyes went round the room, unhurried, taking the measure of every face they crossed. "I've been to ten towns before this one, gentlemen, on the same trail, and I know to the dollar what that old man's passage looked like everywhere else. So I ask this town what I asked the others. Who paid for the headstone?"

(re-hook) Understand what every man in that saloon was thinking in the silence that followed — because they were all thinking the same thing. An attorney. An estate. Six hundred miles. The words rich relations formed over that room like weather, and every soul in it began, at the same instant, to revise his memory of April. And before one honest tongue could move, Vernon Price — who could smell money through a strongbox at forty rods — was on his feet with his thumbs in his silver-buttoned vest. Stay with me now. Don't you move. This is the scene you came for, and it's going to be paid off in brass.

"Sir," said Vernon Price, "I am glad you've come — chairman of the council, Vernon Price, the hotel is mine, you're most welcome in it. And I'm proud to say you've reached the end of your search." The smile came out, the civic one, the one for investors. "What was done for that poor old fellow was done by this town, sir — by the charity of Granite Flat, of which I may say I have the honor to be an instrument. The stone, the plot, the Christian burial — the town saw to it. We are not a place, whatever you may have found elsewhere, that lets a stranger go to a pauper's ditch." He gestured, warm and large, taking in the room, the street, the hill. "If there are — particulars — you wish to discuss, touching the estate, my office is at your service. I kept a fatherly eye on the whole sad business personally."

The room held still. Thirty men who knew better looked into their glasses, and not one of them spoke, because Price held paper on half of them and custom on the rest — and that silence, that purchased silence, was its own kind of testimony, though only one man in the room could read it.

Mr. Quincey Adair looked at Vernon Price for a long, dry, spectacled moment.

"The town," he repeated. "How generous. Then you'll be able to tell me, Mr. Price — since you kept a fatherly eye on it personally — what the stone says."

You could hear the stove tick.

Vernon Price's smile did not fall; it fixed. Because Vernon Price had never once climbed the hill to read a tramp's marker — he'd been up the hill exactly twice, once to price the slope for his investors and once to tell a man in a grave-hole to mind the town's plans — and in that instant, thirty faces watching, he had to gamble. He gambled well; give him that. He was a fluent man. "It says — as I recall — 'Rest in Peace,'" he said, "'a Stranger, Known to God.' Simple and fitting. We didn't feel it our place to invent particulars the poor soul couldn't confirm."

"Mm," said Adair. He had a little leather book out now, though he didn't look at it. "Close. There are four lines, Mr. Price, and you have given me the middle two, which any man could guess. It's the last line the estate is interested in. The last line is why I've come six hundred miles." He looked up, and his dry voice went quieter, and harder. "The man who chose that line knew something about the deceased that was true and that no living person in this town was ever told. I'll have his name now. Not the town's name. His."

Silence. Price's gamble had one more card in it — a fluent man can always claim to have delegated: "the actual inscription, of course, I left to the workman—" — but the card never got played, because a chair scraped at the back of the saloon, and the livery boy stood up. Seventeen years old, ears burning red, wages owed to a Price barn, and he stood up anyway, because he'd carried a corner of that stone up the hill and some debts weigh more than wages.

"It weren't the town." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "It weren't Mr. Price. Weren't nobody but Abel Thorne, and everybody in this room knows it and half of you watched him do it through the window like it was a show." The boy's fists were balled at his sides. "He built the coffin from his paying stock. He dressed him in his own Sunday coat. He walked to the county seat and gave Hobb the mason his last twelve dollars for the granite, and he carved it nine nights with his own hands, and this town —" the boy's voice went to pieces and he finished it anyway — "this town stood at his window and laughed at him. Me too. And the old man died on HIS cot, in HIS blanket, because when he come here sick in April there weren't another door in Granite Flat that'd have him. I turned him out of my own straw. I know who turned him off this porch." He looked straight at Vernon Price. "So does he."

Every eye in the saloon went to Price, and what they saw was a man doing sums — and for the first time in the history of Granite Flat, coming up short. And Mr. Quincey Adair closed his little book with a snap that sounded like a gavel.

"Thank you, son. That tallies." He took up his hat. "In ten towns, gentlemen, I have been offered eleven claimants for my dead client's gratitude, and the truth in each place was a locked church and a priced door — I have the letters he mailed me from every one of them, in his own hand, and Granite Flat's letter, I will tell you, mentions two people only." He turned at the door. "I'm told the undertaker's is the last building on the street. Don't trouble to point the way. It appears to be the one door in this town a man can find in the dark."

(re-hook) Now — while the lawyer's boots go down that dark boardwalk, and the saloon behind him breaks into the loudest quiet argument Granite Flat ever had, and Vernon Price stands holding a smile with nothing left under it — up the street, in the lamplit workshop, a big slow man who knows nothing of any of this is at his bench, winding a brass watch, because he promised. Here it comes. Stay for the watch. Stay for the reading of the will. You have earned the next five minutes, and so, God knows, has he.

Abel answered the knock with wood shavings on his sleeves, and took the lawyer for a bereaved customer, and said what he always said, gentle: "Come in. Sit. Take your time." And Quincey Adair, who had watched rich men and poor men across six hundred miles of this errand, stepped into the sawdust-smelling lamplight, looked at the big homely man, and asked his question one last time — though by now it wasn't a question.

"Mr. Thorne. Who paid for the headstone?"

"I did," said Abel. Plain as weather. "He had nobody. That's all the story there is."

"It is not quite all," said Adair, "but the rest of it is mine to tell." And he set his leather case on the workbench among the shavings, and undid the straps. "In April you buried a man this town knew as a beggar. His name — sit down, Mr. Thorne, I find men prefer to be sitting — his name was Amos Caldwell." He watched the name land; it landed on honest bewilderment, nothing else, and the last check-mark in the lawyer's mind went quietly into its box, because that name had made ten towns' worth of liars flinch with recognition and greed, and the one man it was worth a fortune to had plainly never heard it. "Amos Caldwell of the Caldwell Consolidated silver mines, Mr. Thorne. Amos Caldwell of the Merchants' Bank of Denver, of thirty thousand acres of grazing land, of more money, sir, speaking as the man who kept his books, than this county in its entirety will handle in fifty years. My client was the richest man in the territory. And for the last year of his life he was a dying man walking through little towns in a patched coat, mailing me letters, on his own last errand — to see, before the end, whether his kind ever tells the truth about what a man is worth."

Abel sat down.

"He buried a son once," Adair went on, quieter. "Daniel. A mine of his own killed the boy, and his wife followed inside the year, and my client — I will say this once and deny saying it — was the loneliest human being I have known in forty years of a profession that acquaints a man with loneliness. He wrote a will before he walked. I hold it. It is four sentences long, and I have carried it through eleven towns, and it instructs me to find where he lies and how he was treated, and it leaves the entire estate —" the lawyer's dry voice did not change, which somehow made it land harder — "to whoever showed his body dignity, expecting nothing. Those are its words, Mr. Thorne. Expecting nothing. He was very particular about those words, and I have spent five weeks making certain of them."

The lamp hissed. The big hands on the workbench lay still among the shavings.

"I don't want it," is what Abel Thorne finally said — the first thing he said, and Adair would tell the story for the rest of his life. "It was never for pay. If I take a fortune for it, then it was for pay all along, and it — changes what it was. Backwards." He shook his head slowly, a man working it out. "It was the last free thing anybody ever did for him. I'd sooner it stayed that way."

And Quincey Adair, attorney, looked at the poorest man in Granite Flat turning down the richest estate in the territory on a point of grammar, and for the first time in six hundred miles, he smiled. "He said you'd say that," said Adair. "I am instructed, in that event, to show you the watch."

Abel's hand went to his vest pocket. Of course it did.

"He wrote me from this town, the week he died. His last letter." Adair drew out a page in a thin wild hand and read it plain: "'The one who has my watch is the one. Tell him: he kept it wound. Tell him a debt held by a dead man cannot be refused, and I hold his. He told me once that nobody goes into the ground alone. Neither shall he go back to an empty house, if brass and paper can prevent it. Open the case, Abel.'" The lawyer looked up. "It's engraved, Mr. Thorne. He had it done thirty years ago. I was there."

And Abel Thorne, by lamplight, with shaking big fingers, thumbed open the back of the brass watch he had wound every night for five weeks, and read, in small worn letters:

TO AMOS FROM HIS SON DANIEL —
TIME IS THE ONLY WAGE. SPEND IT KIND.

He was a long while reading four short lines. And then the undertaker of Granite Flat put his face into his big careful hands, at his own workbench, in the sawdust, and wept — for the old man, and for Daniel Caldwell whom he'd never met, and for Lila, and for every soul he'd ever stood over alone with his hat off — while the lawyer stood quiet in the lamplight and let him, because Quincey Adair had learned in a long career the same thing Abel had learned in his: that there is a kind of grief that is only love with no place left to be put, and you do not interrupt it.

[CLOSING]

The will was read out publicly three days later, from the steps of the county clerk's office, to the largest crowd Granite Flat ever assembled, and I will not pretend to you that the town took its medicine gracefully, because towns never do. But it took it. It had no choice; the letters were read too — Amos Caldwell's own account, in his own hand, of the Price Hotel porch, and the saloon stove, and the locked church, and the one lamplit door at the end of the street. Vernon Price stood at the edge of that crowd hearing his own April words read back to him by a lawyer's dry voice — nothing empties a lobby like the smell of poverty — and whatever a man's silver buttons are worth, they will not buy that back out of a town's memory. His investors read about it in the city papers, which got the story wrong in every particular except the ones that mattered, and decided Granite Flat was not, after all, the jewel of the county. The hotel plan died. The council found its spine, or at least its weathervane, and the burial contract stayed where it was, and the pauper field on the hill stayed where it was, and stays there yet.

And Abel Thorne, the richest man in the territory, changed almost nothing, which is exactly why everything changed.

He kept the workshop. He kept the rule. He buried Granite Flat's dead for eleven more years and never again had to look at a coffee tin in March and choose between linen and the winter. The money went out of him the way water goes out of a spring — quietly, and mostly downhill, to where it was needed: a doctor for the town, paid to treat first and bill after, so that fewer people would die the way Lila died, of fever and of fear; a rest home in the county seat for old men off the roads, with a stove and a cot and no questions, called Daniel House, and if you ever wondered who it was named for, now you know; and a standing account at Hobb's mason yard, so that no grave in that county, pauper or prince, ever again went into the ground without a name on stone — or, where no name could be found, without the four lines Abel had carved by lamplight, which the masons came to call, simply, the stranger's verse, and cut so many times they knew it the way other men know a psalm.

He married Cora Quinn in the spring, in the church that had been locked one April night and was not locked now, and the whole town came — came the way towns do when they are trying to say a thing they are too ashamed to say in words — and Abel, who knew exactly what they were saying, let them say it, because he wasn't bitter. He was just changed, and he had been changed a long time, and it had finally stopped costing him.

He kept the watch wound every night of his life. Cora wound it after him.

And up on the hill, on the southeast corner where the light comes long in the evening and you can see the creek, there is a granite stone with four uneven lines, and beside it, by the estate's instruction and the lawyer's own hand, a second stone, small, that says only CALDWELL — because the richest man in the territory lies in a pauper's field by his own choice, in another man's Sunday coat, next to the only door that opened. Quincey Adair came back every year until he died to see it kept right. It was always kept right.

The frontier was full of towns that priced everything, and they are mostly gone now, blown out like lamps, and no one grieves them. But here and there it was full, too, of men and women nobody counted — a slow undertaker, a seamstress with steady eyes, a livery boy who stood up in a saloon with his wages on the line — who understood the one piece of arithmetic that Vernon Price's kind never learns: that what you spend on someone who can never repay you is the only money you keep. Amos Caldwell walked eleven towns to find somebody who knew it. He needed to see it once before he died. And he did — at the end of the street, behind the last door, where the lamp was lit.
